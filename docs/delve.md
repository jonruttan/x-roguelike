# The Delve

The world the `delve` and `kettle` sets share: a dale that holds its
water, the town that grew on it, the Guild that runs the town, the old
people who kept the water before them, and the stone under it all.

This file is the truth of that world.  The game's text follows it:
where they differ, the text is wrong, and the change is listed under
**To bring the text into line**.  All of it is original to
x-roguelike: no names, people, places or text from any other game or
book.

**Spoilers.**  This file says what happened in the world and why, which
is what the game asks a player to work out.  Each section below is
folded shut; open one only if you mean to read it.

## What compels

<details>
<summary>Spoilers. Open to read.</summary>

Narrative is the backbone.  People explore a world to find out what
happened in it and to take a hand in what happens next; everything a
set adds serves that.  An errand is a piece of the story, a page is a
witness, a map is evidence: a shuttered door says someone left, a cold
chimney says the pit stopped.  A mechanic that tells nothing about the
world earns its place some other way or goes.

### Three ages

The world is three ages laid one on another, in the rock and in the
people.  Going down the Delve goes back through them; so does walking
out from Kettle's square to the rim.

| Age | The stone is | Below | Above |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mercantile**: the Guild, now | a commodity with a price | the Galleries: the pit | Kettle and its farms, on the coal measures |
| **Animistic**: the well-keepers, long gone | kin: kept, and carried | the Old Works: the well-keepers' stair | the rim: their city's stones on the limestone |
| **Archaic**: before anyone | itself: what draws the water | the Well-House: the stone in its pool | the bog and the wild hills |

The Flood lies between the Galleries and the Old Works, where the pit
broke into the well-keepers' stair.  It belongs to no age: it is the
breach itself, the mercantile age's water pouring into the animistic
age's works.

### Ways of living

The three ages are what the stone is to people.  Within them, people
have lived four ways, each grown out of the one before, and two more
are coming.  Each way has left its marks, its places and its
adversaries.

| Way | Who | When | What it left | Where it is now | Its adversaries |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tribal** | the herders who first came for the rim's grazing | before the city | rags on thorns, the old numbers | the hills and the High Moss; the hill folk are what is left of it | none of its own yet (see **Ideas**) |
| **Communal** | the well-keepers, keeping the stone in common as kin | the long keeping | the stair, the channels, the well mouth, the ossuary | the Well-House and the Old Works | old 'uns, the ossuary priest |
| **City-state** | the well-keepers' city on the rim | its height, to the procession | walls and vaults, and the keepers built at its end | the rim and the Old Works' vaults; the Plain's towns keep the form | rust golems, the warder, glisters, the Old Man |
| **Mercantile** | Kettle's settlers up the Seethe, the Guild, and the League's towns and banks | three hundred years ago, to now | the drained floor, the charter cut into the old people's stone, the market, the pit, the terraces, the Tip, the White Church | Kettle, its farms and the Galleries; the Plain's towns | the fireman, gallery shades, salvagers, the Guild's bailiffs, distrainers, the League's agents, Rot-hunters, salt-runners |
| **Colonial** | nobody yet | coming | its first sign: the League buying sweet water from the dales | the Plain, reaching | none yet |
| **Industrial** | nobody yet | coming | its first signs: the engine house, the furnace, the brickfield | the Plain first, where the League has the powder and the money | none yet |

Before them all is the archaic, with no people in it: the stone, its
pool, dripstone, the old bear and the pool-born.

The mercantile way is now.  Kettle trades within a packhorse's reach:
coal, wool and leather go over the Nick, salt comes back, and dried
fish comes up from the coast.  Nobody carries goods farther than that,
and nobody rules a place they cannot ride to.  The League reaches into
the dales by credit, bought charters and hired men, not by holding
them.  The Guild borrowed the Plain's form to borrow its money.

Two ways are coming, and the holdwater would bring both:

- **Colonial**: the Plain taking what a dale has and carrying it off to
  the centre.  The League buying sweet water is its beginning; selling
  the holdwater east would be the act in full.
- **Industrial**: the dale is approaching it, with one engine bought on
  credit, one furnace, horses at the gins, and no powder.  The engine
  was industry's first foothold, and when it broke the dale fell back
  on horses, peat and charcoal.  Industry would come from the Plain
  with the League's powder and money, and the holdwater's price would
  pay for it.

### What each way left in words

The dale speaks the settlers' tongue, and only that: the speech of a
northern dale and its pit, never another country's.  A trade or a
thing the pits and dales have a word for goes by that word: the
fireman who tests for gas, the lampman in the lamp room, the
furnaceman, the banksman.  Each way of living left words in it, and
traditions kept by people who have forgotten the reasons.
The oldest words are on the oldest things: water and heights.

- **Tribal**: names nobody can explain, on waters and heights.  The
  hill folk call the river the Oune and the High Moss Harrack, and count
  their sheep unna, tava, tirra, sorra, pell.  The rags tied by springs
  are theirs.
- **Communal**: words for keeping and kin.  The hill folk call a spring
  "a mother", and "holdwater" is the settlers' rendering of their tale
  of "the water that holds".  A child's first drink is from the Well
  Stone, "first water"; Kettle still keeps that, and the White Church
  gives salt instead.
- **City-state**: words of measure and order.  The hill folk measure a
  climb in flights, the rise of a stair between landings, and call a
  walled yard a court.  At midsummer they walk the line of the old wall
  round the rim, "walking the wall", not knowing it was a city's.  The
  marks are the city's writing: pictures, copied now on cloth.
- **Mercantile, the settlers'**: the dale's everyday speech: beck,
  clough, force, moss, edge, nick, hagg and scar, and the names they
  gave: the Seethe, Kettle, Quern Edge.  The moot, the charter, the
  market, and the chapel's light on the river.
- **Mercantile, the Plain's**: words that came with money and the
  church: guild, ledger, bailiff, credit, distraint; the Rot; salt
  oaths, and bread and salt for a guest.
- **The pit**: the newest words: firedamp, gin, banksman, hewer, putter,
  the Beam, the Tip, the Tally.  The fireman and his craft came from
  Halmere's salt miners.

### Three questions

Everything in the world answers three questions, and a thing that
cannot is not ready to ship:

1. **Why is it there in the first place?**
2. **Why has it become what it is?**
3. **How is it used now?**

The well-head, for one: (1) where the stone's water rises through a
fissure in the basin floor, a spring that never fails; (2) the
well-keepers cut it a stone mouth, and Kettle's settlers cut their
charter into that stone because it was the one thing everyone shared;
(3) a pump-head the Guild keeps and the town draws from, and the one
place the charter is judged, so it is where the ledger must be laid.

## The world beyond the dale

<details>
<summary>Spoilers. Open to read.</summary>

Mobility is poor and power does not reach far.  Nobody can hold the
hills with soldiers; what reaches into a dale is money, paper and
hired men.

- **The sea** lies west.  The rain comes off it and falls on the hills.
- **The Dales**: upland basins in the hills, each with its own water,
  its own market town and its own charter.  A dale governs itself by
  custom: its charter is cut on its well-stone, and disputes are judged
  there before the town.  The law holds as long as the town stands
  behind it.
- **The passes** cross the hills east.  Packhorse trains, carriers,
  drovers and pedlars use them; news travels as fast as a pedlar
  walks.  The passes close at the first snow.
- **The Plain** lies east, in the hills' rain shadow: rich, crowded and
  dry.  Its towns are city-states in a loose league, with real guilds,
  bankers and lawyers.  The League keeps powder to itself, so the hills
  fight with bolts and blades and blast no rock.  It projects power the
  cheap way: credit, charters bought, and bailiffs hired.
- **Salt.**  Under the Plain lie thick beds of rock salt, left by a sea
  that dried there long before anyone.  Salt is the Plain's wealth: its
  towns grew over the salt, and the League grew from the salt trade.
  Salt goes west over the passes by packhorse on the saltways, because
  every dale needs it for its meat and its hides; coal, wool and
  leather come back east.  The Plain's salt mines are old and deep, and
  generations of miners have carved chambers in them into chapels.
- **The dry years.**  The Plain's wells are failing, and not only for
  want of rain: drawn deeper every year, they pull up brine from the
  salt beds, and a town's well turns salt and is lost for good.  The
  League buys sweet water where it can, and the dales are where the
  sweet water is.  Salt for water is the oldest bargain between hill
  and plain.

### The salt under the Plain: Halmere

Halmere is the League's oldest salt town, over the shallowest of the
beds; the League began there.  Under it is **the Hall**, as its
miners call their mine.

- **The Hall** is older than Kettle.  Its galleries are timbered with
  hill oak, carried east over the passes.  Its chambers are the
  rooms the salt was cut from.  In some of them the miners have carved
  chapels in the salt, with figures and lamps of salt crystal, and these
  are the White Church's holiest places (see **Faith**).  Brine lakes
  stand in old chambers and are crossed by boat.  Horses live their
  whole lives below, walking the treadwheels that wind the salt up.
  There is gas in the salt too.  Halmere's firemen burn it off
  before each shift, and Kettle learned that craft from them when the
  Guild took the Plain's form.  The sick are brought down to breathe the
  Hall's dry air.
- **Why it is there**: the beds lie nearest the surface at Halmere, and
  a salt spring rose there.  **What it became**: a mine, then a town's
  wealth, then the League's, then the White Church's holy ground.  **How
  it is used now**: it is still worked, while the town above it goes dry.
  Halmere's wells have turned salt, and the League's buyers, who would
  pay anything for sweet water, are Halmere's.
- **The stone in the Hall.**  Laid under Halmere, the holdwater would
  draw a country's water to it through the salt beds, and water
  dissolves salt.  Where the town's wells draw, the Hall already weeps:
  brine runs down its walls, and its miners fear water above all
  things.  Nobody on the Plain has yet put the two together.
- **Salt for coal.**  The dale buys its salt with its coal.  No coal has
  gone east since the pumps stopped, so little salt is coming west, and
  a dale without salt cannot cure its meat or its hides for the winter.

Kettle is one dale among several, the first a set visits.  Every dale
had its well-keepers once; their stones, rag-trees and marks are
everywhere in the hills.  Whether every dale has a stone of its own is
the long mystery under every set.

## The ground

<details>
<summary>Spoilers. Open to read.</summary>

The rock is ordinary, and so are the weather and the life on it.  The
stone is the one thing in the dale physics does not explain.

### Rock

A basin, as such country forms: the youngest rock in the middle, older
rock coming up round it.  Walking out from the square crosses all three.

- **The coal measures** fill the middle: sandstone, shale, coal seams,
  fireclay and ironstone.  Kettle, its farms and its pit stand on them.
  Mercantile.
- **Gritstone** rises round them as the hills: coarse sandstone,
  millstone rock, which weathers to edges and boulders.  It holds water
  and gives it out in springs at its foot; on the flat tops the rain
  stands as blanket bog.  Wild.
- **Limestone** lies under both and comes up at the rim, all round the
  dale: bare scars, short sweet grass, sinkholes and caves.  Water
  sinks into it and runs in the dark.  The well-keepers lived on it and
  built in it.  Animistic.
- **The stone** lies at the foot of the limestone, under the middle of
  the basin, in a pool in a cave: dark and glassy, always cold, beaded
  with water in dry air, the size of a loaf and heavy as iron.
  Archaic.

### The stone's rule

The stone takes water in and gives it out where it lies.

- Ground water within its reach runs toward it, through the rock as if
  downhill; its reach is the dale and the hills round it.  This is the
  "drinking".
- What it takes in, it gives out where it lies.  Under the dale that is
  its pool, and the pool rises up a fissure to the spring in the basin
  floor: the well-head.
- So the dale is wet in a drought: its water comes from everything the
  hills ever soaked up, gathered to the stone, not from this month's
  rain.  And the Plain is dry partly because of it: water that would
  run east under the hills runs to the stone instead.
- **Moved,** the stone takes in ahead of it and gives out behind.  A
  flooded stair opens before whoever carries it, and the water rises
  after them.
- **Laid somewhere else,** it draws the country round its new place.
  The old ground water follows it, slowly, over seasons.  Carried over
  the pass, the Plain would get a spring and the dale's wells would
  fail.  Laid anywhere in the dale, the dale keeps its water.
- **Under the Brand** it drinks harder.  It answers the fire in the sky
  by drawing water, as the grotto's mark says: fire given from above,
  water drawn from below.  Its reach grows, ground water runs to it
  from farther off, and springs and wells fail across the hills and
  the Plain while the dale's well runs as it always has.  This is why
  the Brand's years are years of need.

Nothing else in the dale is uncanny of itself.  **The uncanny goes where
the stone's water goes**: dead that do not lie still, stone that walks,
light from the rock.  Before the breach that was only the well-keepers'
works and the stone's pool.  Since the breach, the stone's water runs
through the pit.

### Water

- Rain on the hills runs off in becks to the river, and soaks into the
  grit and the limestone.
- The river crosses the basin and leaves by a gap in the west, to the
  sea.  The mill race and the weir are on it, and the ferry where it
  runs deep below the town.
- The spring at the well-head rises in the middle of the basin floor
  and runs down to the river.  In a drought it is the one water that
  never fails.
- The basin floor was a mire over clay until it was drained.  The
  shallow wells in Kettle's yards draw from the coal measures, not from
  the stone, and they can fail.

### Fire: the Candle

The coal measures breathe firedamp: the gas of the coal, which seeps up
wherever the shale is broken.  In a clough on the valley side a beck
falls over a ledge of shale, Candle Force, and behind the fall the gas
comes out of the rock in a small grotto.  A flame burns there, lit
long ago and never out since: **the Candle**.

1. **Why there**: the beck cut down through the shale and opened a
   crack the gas rises through, and lightning lit it.  No hand lit the
   Candle and nobody made it: the sky lit it, the rock feeds it, and
   water falls over it.  The tribal herders saw it happen, and it is
   their oldest tale, "the night the sky lit the fall".
2. **What it became**: the well-keepers kept it as the dale's breath,
   fire living under falling water, the stone's other half, and tied
   rags by it as by their springs.  Kettle's folk keep the custom
   without the reason; lovers and the newly widowed go there.  Miners
   read it: when the Candle burns tall, the seams are gassy, and a
   careful fireman stops a shift.
3. **How it is used now**: it has burned taller than anyone remembers
   since the pumps stopped.  The drowning pit's water rises in the
   workings and drives their gas out through the rock, and the Candle
   shows it.  Nobody in Kettle doubts what that means.  The Guild says
   it is the drought.

**How the Candle is read.**  The fireman walked up Candle Clough
every morning before the shift, and the town still goes up to look.
Two things stand it up tall:

- **The sky.**  When the air's weight falls before a storm, more gas
  comes out of the coal, and the Candle stands up.  The hill folk say
  "when the Candle stands up, bring the sheep down", and a careful
  fireman stops a shift.  It is the dale's glass.
- **The water.**  Water rising in the workings drives their gas out to
  it.

So a tall Candle in settled weather means the pit.  A drought is
settled weather, when the Candle should burn low; it burns taller than
ever, which is how every shepherd knows the Guild's "it is the drought"
for a lie.  Each faith reads the Candle in its own way (see **Faith**).
The hill folk say the dale ends the day it goes out.

**The Candle speaks.**  It breathes the air of the rock the pit is cut
in, so whatever moves the air below moves the flame: a fall of roof, a
door opened in the workings, the water rising.  Since the breach, the
stone's water drives that gas, so the Candle carries what the stone's
water touches, and the uncanny with it.  It is the one place the
worlds speak to each other: the sky above, the pit and the stone
below, the living and the dead.

**The grotto** keeps a mark from every age: the herders' rags on the
thorns, a well-keeper mark cut in the rock, the settlers' coins in the
pool and oaths scratched beside them, and the names of the firedamp
shift, scratched by their widows.  The well-keeper mark is a jagged
line over a flame over waves: sky, fire, water.  It is half a message;
the mark at the fourth stair is the other half.  Read together: what
was given from above is kept below.

It is the same gas as the pit's gas motes and the blast that killed the
firedamp shift: the mercantile age's danger is the animistic age's
holy flame.

### The air

The air lies in layers, as the rock does.  From below:

- **The pit's damps.**  Firedamp gathers in the roof; it is light, and
  it burns.  Blackdamp pools in low workings; it is heavy, and puts a
  lamp out before it kills a man, so a lamp going dim is the warning.
  Afterdamp is what a firing leaves, and it killed most of the firedamp
  shift.  With the furnace shaft cold, all three are gathering below.
- **The kettle's air.**  Cold pools on the floor at night, fog rises off
  the wet ground in the morning, and in a working winter smoke lies
  under the cold.
- **The tops' wind**: the westerlies off the sea, and the rain they
  carry.
- **The sky**: lightning, sun, moon and stars.

### Weather and sun

- Wet westerlies; the hills take the rain and the Plain lies in their
  shadow.
- The kettle is a frost hollow: cold air pools in the basin, so late
  frosts, cold nights even in summer, and morning fog rising off the
  wet floor: the kettle steaming, which is where the name comes from.
  In a winter with the pit working, coal smoke lies under the cold air.
- A northern latitude: low sun, long summer evenings, short winter days.
- This summer is a drought: no rain for a month, the becks low, the
  river thin.  The becks are falling faster than a dry month explains:
  the Brand is up, and the stone is drinking harder.  The well-head
  runs as it always has.

### Soil

- The hills: thin acid soil and peat.  Peat is the shepherds' fuel.
- The basin floor: heavy clay, a mire until drained; now fields and
  hay meadow.
- The rim: short sweet grass on the limestone, the best grazing in the
  dale, which is why the well-keepers lived there.

### Plants

- The hills: heather, gorse, bilberry and bracken; cotton-grass and
  sphagnum in the bog; rowan and birch in the cloughs.
- The farms: hawthorn and blackthorn hedges, oats and barley, hay.
- The valley sides: oak wood; alder and willow by the river and the race.
- The rim: ash in the scars, flowers in the cracks of the bare rock, and
  yews by the well-keepers' graves.
- The town: soot on the walls; kale, leeks and onions in the yards.

### Animals

- The hills: hill sheep, curlew and lapwing, hares, foxes, adders on
  the dry heath, and in the oak woods boar and a few wolves, for the
  bog and the passes keep people out of the uplands.
- The valley: rooks and crows by the hundred; trout in the becks; eels
  in the river, come up from the sea as elvers.
- The pit: rats, toads, feral ferrets bred from the miners' ratters,
  and firedamp, which is no animal but kills like one.
- The caves of the rim: bats, blind white crayfish, shrimp.  Their
  kin live in the stone's pool and, since the breach, in the Flood.

### What each age builds with

- **Archaic**: nothing built.  The stone, its pool, the caves, the bog,
  the gas in the shale.
- **Animistic**: dressed limestone laid without mortar; corbelled
  vaults; channels cut to lead rain down to the stone; carved well
  mouths; graves under yews; marks cut in stone, mostly pictures.  Their
  craft was water and stone: rods and rings steeped in the stone's
  water, bog iron from the moss, and keepers built of stone that the
  water moves, bound with that iron.
- **Mercantile**: gritstone for the engine house and the Guild hall,
  fireclay brick and stone-slate roofs for the terraces, iron smelted
  from the coal measures' own ironstone, black shale spoil heaps,
  pumps and rails, and soot over all of it.

## The history

<details>
<summary>Spoilers. Open to read.</summary>

Spans are rough; nobody in the dale keeps a calendar older than the
charter.

1. **Before anyone.**  The stone lies in its pool at the foot of the
   limestone, drawing the water of the hills round it.  Its spring
   makes the basin floor a mire, and the kettle steams.  Gas seeps from
   the shale.
2. **The well-keepers** (a thousand years ago or more).  Herders come
   for the rim's grazing and the spring that never fails.  They follow
   the rim's caves down and find the stone in its pool.  They take it
   for kin, the dale's mother, and keep it: they dress the caves into a
   long stair from the rim down to the pool, the Well-House, cut
   channels to lead the rain down to it, and cut a mouth for its spring
   in the mire.  They keep the Candle lit.  Their city stands on the
   rim.  They bury their dead under yews there, and in time carry the
   bones down to the ossuary beside the pool, to lie near the stone.
3. **The procession.**  The Brand stands in the west.  In a great drought
   their kin over the pass are dying of thirst.  The well-keepers do
   what kin do: they carry the stone over the pass in procession, as
   one carries a god to visit.  The water follows it.  Their own spring
   fails, their wells fail, and the city scatters.  Its last keepers
   fetch the stone home and lay it back in its pool.  They know the
   Brand comes back, some three hundred years apart, and that someone
   will be tempted under it again; what they build is for that.  They
   wall the Well-House, build keepers of stone that walks to stand in
   it against the next Brand, and cut the mark at the fourth stair:
   while the stone lies here the dale has wells; lift it and the water
   follows.  Beside the mark they begin a count, a tailed star for each
   return.  The water comes back; the city does not.  Its people are
   the scattered hill folk whose shepherds still tie rags on thorns by
   springs, not knowing why.
4. **Kettle** (some three hundred years ago).  Settlers come up the
   river into the basin for the spring.  The floor is mire.  Those who
   drain it, with ditches, then wind pumps, then horse pumps, become
   the drainers.  The town's charter is cut into the well-keepers'
   well-mouth: the water of the well is common to all; the drainers
   keep the wells and the floor dry, and in return may work the ground
   they drain and take what lies beneath it.  "What lies beneath" meant
   clay, stone and peat.  Disputes over the wells are judged at the
   well-head before the town.  The charter calls the old marks on the
   stone "the holdwater's marks", from a tale the settlers had off the
   hill folk; no one believes the tale.  The Brand stood over the dale
   the year the settlers came, and the hill folk told the tale under it.
   That is why the charter's first line makes the well's water common
   to all, cut into the stone's own mouth: the settlers did not believe
   the tale, and cut the line anyway.  The charter is the town's answer
   to the last Brand.
5. **Coal** (some hundred and twenty years ago).  Digging the drains
   turns up coal.  The drainers read "what lies beneath" as the coal,
   and the town lets them, because only the drainers can keep a pit dry
   in so wet a basin.  To borrow from the Plain they take the form of
   the Plain's guilds: the Pump Guild of Kettle.  Coal goes over the
   pass by packhorse and is burned in the dale's hearths, its forge and
   its pumps.
6. **The engine house** (some forty years ago).  The Guild builds a
   steam pumping engine on credit from a League bank, and the pit goes
   deeper.  The mine's drainage pulls down the spring's head, so the
   Guild puts a pump on the well-head: the town now draws its common
   water through the Guild's iron.  One spring, the sky falling before a
   storm, the Candle stands up tall and the fireman does not stop the
   shift; firedamp kills it in the lower galleries, and the town buries
   what it can find.
7. **The seam fails** (the last few years).  The Guild owes the League
   more than the pit will ever pay.  It keeps the miners on with talk
   of a new seam and drives the lower drift deeper.
8. **The breach** (this summer, the ninth of the month).  The east
   drift breaks into dressed stone, a wall laid without mortar, and a
   dry passage running down: the well-keepers' stair, where it passes
   under the pit.  The foreman posts two lamps at the hole and sends
   word up.
9. **The water.**  The breach opens a short way from the hills to the
   stone.  The coal measures' water now runs to the breach and down,
   through every gallery on the way: the sumps rise in a drought.  The
   Brand rose the same month, and under it the stone drinks harder, so
   more water comes through the workings each week than the engine was
   built to lift.  The Guild cuts coal to the pumps by a third, since coal is money owed
   east, and orders them run harder.  The shallow wells in Kettle's
   yards begin to fall.
10. **The first order.**  The Guild sends six down past the breach to
    find where the water goes: Hollis from the engine house, the
    Guild's surveyor, the lampman, Tam and two miners.  The foreman
    goes with them.
11. **The report.**  The surveyor levels the stair and finds the water
    going down it.  At the fourth stair he finds the mark and reads its
    pictures.  He sends a fair report up by one of the miners, and keeps
    his field notes.
12. **The resolution.**  The Guild in council reads the report: a stone
    that gathers a country's water.  The Plain would pay anything for
    it, and the Guild's debt would be gone.  It resolves to sell it east,
    orders the crew to bring it up, and minutes the wells of Kettle as
    "the town's concern, not the Guild's".  It knows what the mark says
    will follow.  That is the charter broken in the Guild's own hand.
13. **The bailiff.**  The Guild's head bailiff sits in council and
    serves its orders; he is sent to carry this one down.  He cannot
    stomach it.  Before he goes he lifts the sealed ledger with the
    minute in it and hides it under the pump-house floor, and tells his
    wife, Ada.  He carries his served copy down, catches the crew up
    below the breach, serves the order, and tells the foreman what it
    means.  On the way he writes to Ada; the letter is never sent.
14. **The engine fails.**  Run harder on a third less coal, without
    Hollis, the old engine breaks.  The pumps stop, the lower galleries

    the outcrops are the Guild's by the charter: cold hearths, a cold

15. **The refusal.**  Nine days below the breach, keepers bar the way

16. **Nobody comes up.**  The men climb into the Flood.  The miner who

## The narrative

<details>
<summary>Spoilers. Open to read.</summary>

The player is a miner of Kettle, kin to one of the crew, out of work
since the pumps stopped.

1. **Kettle.**  The player finds the ledger and lays it on the
   well-head.  Under the charter the Guild has broken its keeping of the
   wells, and loses its charter: the right to work beneath, and the
   pit, pass to the town.  The ledger also tells the town what the
   Guild found and what it means to do.
2. **The Delve.**  The town sends the player down the drowned pit
   after the holdwater, before the Guild's men reach it from the rim.
   Brought up and laid in Kettle's own well-head, it stays in the dale
   under the town's charter.  Whoever carries it climbs through
   stairs the stone drinks open ahead of them, while the water it gives
   out rises after them and puts the breach under a lake.  The pit is
   lost; it was finished anyway.  The Guild's diggers in the old stair
   are flooded out.

The question the world asks: what do we owe to what keeps us alive?
Each age answers it: kin keep it, a company sells it.  The town's
answer is the player's act.  The question the next sets ask is harder:
whose water is it, when the Plain is dry because the dale is wet?

### The long arc: the curse

Kettle knows it is cursed: a spring that never fails, and a dale that
fails anyway.  The long arc asks why.  Each set gives an answer that
is true and is not the last:

1. **Kettle: the Guild.**  Debt, a broken charter, the coal yard sold
   east.  The ledger proves it and the town takes the charter back.
   True, and not why.
2. **The Delve: the stone.**  It drank the pit and it drinks the dale.
   The town keeps it.  Truer, and still not why.
3. **The count: the Brand.**  At the fourth stair each return is a
   tailed star over a picture of who was tempted under it.  The Guild
   is only the latest.  The curse comes round every three hundred
   years.
4. **The bargain: why the stone drinks at all.**  Older than the
   well-keepers.  They found the stone already drinking, and kept it as
   kin without knowing what it drank for.  Their procession east is the
   first return the count records, not the first bargain.  Who struck
   it, with what, and what it cost are left open.

Each faith already names the curse, so the town has rival answers,
and each is partly right:

- **The White Church:** the Rot.
- **The chapel:** the Giver's warning.
- **The hill folk:** the sky's fire, come looking.
- **The Listeners:** the fall, coming again.

The player learns which part of each is right.  Under the curse is the
world's question, what we owe to what keeps us alive: a place that
holds what everyone needs is taken in every age, and every taking
leaves it worse.

## Faith

<details>
<summary>Spoilers. Open to read.</summary>

Two established churches, and the old ways under both.  Each people
reveres what keeps it alive, and each church has made adversaries of
what the earlier ages revered.  So every adversary deep below the dale
is of an earlier age, and each faith has its own name for it.  The
mercantile age's adversaries are people: the Guild, its bailiffs and
hired men, road thieves, and the League's buyers (see **From time to
time**).

### The White Church

The League's church, established on the Plain and paid by its towns.
It teaches that the Plain was a sea; the water went up and the salt
stayed.  Water passes and salt endures, and what is salted does not
rot: meat, hides, souls.  A pinch of salt goes on the tongue at a
birth, at a marriage and at a death.  Oaths are sworn on salt, and a
guest is given bread and salt.  Its holiest places are the chapels
carved in the Hall at Halmere, and pilgrims go down to them.

Its adversary is **the Rot**: standing water, springs that will not
stay put, anything that drinks.  To the White Church the well-keepers'
marks are the Rot's writing, the rag-trees its altars, and the Candle
a heathen fire.  A stone that draws a country's water would be the
Rot itself, and the League's own banks mean to buy it.

In Kettle it is the Guild's church.

### The chapel

The settlers' faith, older in Kettle than the Guild.  The settlers came
up the Seethe from the coast, and their faith is the coast's: the sea
is the Giver's, all water runs home to it, and the dead go down the
water to the Giver.  Graves face west.  For each death a light is set
on the river at dusk.  The miners, the farms and the river end go to
chapel.  The chapel reads the Candle as the light of the dead: tall,
it is the drowned below asking to go down.

Its adversary is what keeps water from going home: drowned ground,
still pools, a thing that holds water back.  The drowned below are
**the dead who cannot go down**.  If the holdwater is real, the chapel
holds it a thief of the Giver's water, though it is also the town's
water.  The chapel has not settled that, and it is the town's question
too.

### The old ways

Older than both churches, and kept under both:

- **The hill folk** tie rags on thorns by springs, leave coin at the
  Candle, plant yews by graves, and count their sheep in old numbers
  nobody can explain.  These are the well-keepers' customs, with the
  reasons lost.
- **Kettle** goes to the Candle: lovers to promise, the newly widowed to
  leave a rag.
- **The miners** do not whistle below, since whistling calls the wind
  down the shaft; they leave a crust for the knocker, touch the Well
  Stone on the way to a shift, and never take a dead man's tally off
  its nail.

The White Church preaches against the old ways, and the chapel looks
the other way.

### The Listeners

An animistic practice, not a church, kept by hill folk and by the
poor end of Kettle, who come down from the tribal herders.  A Listener
carries a flame into one of the rim's side caves, which do not reach
the stair, where water falls in the dark, and sits with it through the
night, watching how it leans.  The caves' ceilings are black with
centuries of their fires.  Their flames
do move, but with the cave's own breath, which follows the weather and
not the stone.  The Listeners know it: the Candle is the one that
speaks, and theirs only listen.  They are trying to make again the
night the sky lit the fall, and they know a flame lit by hand cannot.

The White Church calls them the Rot's worshippers.  The Guild's dig has
driven them out of the rim's caves, and the Rot-hunters come for them
as well as for the Candle.  They are the first to read the Candle for
what it is.

### Speaking with what is unseen

The Candle is the true form: a fire no hand lit, which carries what
moves below to the sky above.  Everything else is an attempt at it, a
flame someone carried in and lit:

- **The cathedral.**  The White Church's chapels carved in the Hall at
  Halmere: fire kindled inside the rock, lamps of salt crystal, figures
  cut where nothing lives.  It is the Plain building a Candle by craft.
  But salt is dead rock: the Hall's air is dry and still, nothing
  breathes through it, and its lamps answer nothing.  It is the
  grandest of the attempts and the emptiest.
- **The Listeners' fires**, in the rim's caves: close, but answering
  only the weather.
- **The chapel's river lights**, set on the Seethe for the dead: an
  attempt that sends and never receives.

### The sky

What each people makes of the weather, the lightning and the stars.

- **The tribal herders**: the sky gives the rain and the stone keeps
  it, and the Candle is what passed between them.  Storms are the sky
  come to see its fire.  The bog lights are sparks that fell short of
  the clough and never found it.
- **The well-keepers**: fire given from above, water drawn from below,
  and the stone between them.  Their mark in the grotto says so.
- **The hill folk, now**: the Candle is their glass.  They cut peat and
  coppice on the waning moon, and walk the wall at midsummer.
- **The miners**: the Candle tells them when the sky is falling, and the
  firedamp shift died on such a day.
- **The chapel**: the water goes home to the sea, rises as cloud, and
  the Giver sends it back as rain.  A drought is the Giver holding back
  for a wrong done, and the chapel preaches this summer's as judgment.
- **The settlers**: coast people still, who name the stars as the sea
  taught them and steer by them.
- **The White Church**: the old sea went up into heaven, so rain is
  heaven's water, pure until it lands and stands, when it is the Rot's.
  Lightning is heaven's judgment.  That heaven's own fire lit the Candle
  is the White Church's embarrassment: it calls that strike a false
  fire, the Rot's, and the Rot-hunters would put the Candle out.

### Signs

What is in the sky now, and what it means.  Each is real, and each
people reads it its own way.  None is settled.

- **The Brand**: a tailed star in the west.  It rose the month of the
  breach and grows each night.  The hill folk say the sky's fire has
  come looking; the Listeners, that the night the sky lit the fall is
  coming again; the White Church, that heaven has drawn a brand against
  the Rot; the chapel, that the Giver is warning; the miners, that it
  is bad.  In the Old Works a well-keeper mark shows a tailed star over
  a procession carrying the stone east.  The Brand has come before, the
  last time the stone was moved.  Nobody above knows how often it comes;
  the fourth stair does.  Beside the mark there, a row of tailed stars
  counts its returns, one for each, some three hundred years apart, and
  under each a picture of who was tempted under it and what became of
  them.  The well-keepers cut the first, the procession; the keepers
  have cut each since.  Between are people turned back on the stair.
  The last is the well-mouth with new lines cut round it: the charter.
  There is room for the next.

  The Brand does not move the stone; it tempts.  Each time it comes,
  whoever knows of the stone is tempted to move it.  The well-keepers
  carried it east under the Brand, and the Guild means to sell it east
  under the Brand again.

  **The Brand's years** are years of deprivation and need, because the
  stone drinks harder under it (see **The stone's rule**): drought,
  failing wells and thirst, everywhere at once.  In ordinary years water
  is a commodity, drawn, carried and traded, salt for water.  In the
  Brand's years it becomes an asset: what is worth having is no longer
  the water but the place it comes from, and whoever holds the stone
  holds a country's water.  That is the temptation.  Every return in
  the count was such a time: the procession, when the well-keepers' kin
  over the pass were dying of thirst; the turnings-back, people driven
  down the stair by need; the settlers, who came up the river for the
  spring that never fails.  Now there is drought in the hills, the
  Plain's wells are failing faster, and the League is buying sweet
  water, and the Guild sees an asset.
- **The Sparks**: falling stars out of the north-east, every late
  summer, so they are falling now.  They are the herders' sparks that
  fell short of the clough, and the hill folk say each one is a bog
  light born.
- **The stones on the rim**: the standing stones line up on where the
  sun rises at midsummer, which is why the hill folk walk the wall
  then.  One row points elsewhere, low in the west, where the Brand
  rose.
- **The sky-fires**: in hard winters the northern sky burns green.  The
  chapel calls them the drowned going home; the hill folk, the old
  people walking the wall in the sky.
- **The darkening**: the White Church keeps the only reckoning of the
  heavens, at Halmere, and foretells the sun darkened at noon this
  midwinter.  It preaches it as the end of the Rot.  Its knowing the
  day ahead is part of its power over the Plain.

## The dale of Kettle

<details>
<summary>Spoilers. Open to read.</summary>

Kettle is a market town of some two thousand people, and the only town
in the dale.  It lives on three things: the farms on the drained floor,
the wool off the hills, and the pit.  The pit came last and grew
biggest.  Nearly every trade in the dale came to work for it, and since
the pumps stopped nearly every trade is idle or short.

Out from the square, each band a rock and an age:

- **Kettle**: the town on the coal measures, its square and well-head
  in the middle, the pit and engine house at one end of the main
  street, the river at the low end.
- **The Farms**: the drained floor; hedged fields, the road.  Farmhouse
  cellars.
- **The Hills**: the gritstone, the gorse and the bog, and the clough
  with Candle Force in it.  Shepherds' bothies, left in a hurry when
  the Guild's men cleared the rim.
- **The Rim**: bare limestone all round the dale, the well-keepers'
  stones, and the Guild's men about them.  The old vaults run down.

### The town

The pithead is at the top of the main street, the square and the
well-head are in the middle, and the river is at the bottom.  The
town is five districts, each a screen at two metres a cell: **the
Square** in the middle; **the Pithead** north of it; **the River End**
south; **the Terraces** east, with the chapel; and **Guild Street**
west, with the Guild hall, the White Church and the Guild's shop.  On
the valley it is one place, its door on the hub, with its own fields
round it.

- **The square.**  The well-head stands in the middle: the
  well-keepers' stone mouth, the charter cut round it, and the Guild's
  iron pump on it.  The moot stands here when the town is called.  On
  market day the farms, the hill folk and the pedlars fill it.
- **The pump-house**, against the well-head, is the Guild's.  The
  pump's rods come up in it, and the ledger is under its floor.
- **The Guild hall**, of gritstone, is the biggest building in the
  town.  It holds the council chamber, the pay office, the strongroom
  and the clerks.
- **The council house**, plainer, stands across the square.  It holds
  the town's own officers, the town surveyor and his levels, and the
  town's copy of the charter.
- **The Guild's shop.**  The Guild paid part of every wage in its own
  tickets, good only here.  The shop is still open and selling dear,
  on credit against wages that no longer come, so the town owes the
  Guild too.
- **The inn** is where the carriers and pedlars stop and the town
  drinks.  Its yard and stables take the packhorse trains.
- **The smithy.**  The smith shoes the horses and makes every iron
  thing the pit uses.  The forge is cold for want of coal.
- **The chandler's** sells tallow candles and lamp oil, rendered from
  the farms' fat, and the smell carries all down the street.
- **The bakehouse** is the town's common oven, where the terraces bake
  their bread.  It burned coal; now it burns what wood it can get, one
  day in three.
- **The chapel and its graveyard.**  This is the miners' chapel, plain
  stone near the terraces.  A long row of stones there marks the
  firedamp shift, every one with the same day on it: the Long Row.
- **The White Church**, beside the Guild hall.  The Guild built it of
  gritstone when it took the Plain's form, and the Guild's families
  worship there.
- **The terraces** are the miners' rows below the pithead: brick under
  stone-slate, a yard behind each with a shallow well and kale.  Doors
  are shuttered where families have gone over the pass to look for
  work.
- **The Guild houses** are stone, near the square, with gardens.
- **The watch house** stands where the road comes into the town.

### The pithead

At the top of the main street, the pit's own ground:

- **The engine house**: gritstone, tall, with its beam out through the

- **The engine shaft**, where the pumps hang.  Its water stands at the

- **The winding shaft**, with the horse gin over it: a great drum the

  down.
- **The furnace shaft**, the upcast.  It has been cold since the fire at

- **The pit office**, with the daybook, the pay lists and the foreman's
  room.
- **The lamp room**, where the lampman gave out every light and

- **The sawpit and the timber yard.**
- **The coal yard**, empty: the last of it went east for the debt.  The

- **The spoil heap**, the Tip: black shale, as high as the engine

- **The Guild's furnace**, where ironstone was smelted with the pit's

- **The brickfield**: the clay pit and kilns, at the town's edge by

### The river end

- **The mill** stands on the race above the weir: the town's corn

- **The weir**, with the eel traps beside it.
- **The ropewalk**: a long straight shed along the bank.
- **The tannery** stands downstream of everything, for the smell, with

- **The ferry** crosses below the town, where the river runs deep, on

- **The poor end**, by the water: the eel-catcher, the scrap-picker,

### The farms

- **The closes**: hedged fields on the drained floor, of oats, barley

- **The drains**: dikes and ditches that keep the floor dry and run to

- **The farmsteads**: stone houses with barns, dairies and cellars.
- **The road** from the square, out through the farms and up to the

### The valley sides

- **The oak wood** covers the slopes between the floor and the hills.

- **Charcoal** fed the forge before the pit's coal.  Since the pit
  stopped the burners are the busiest trade in the dale, and they
  cannot make enough.
- **Candle Force**, in its clough, with rags on the thorns and coin in

### The hills

- **The quarry** is on the nearest gritstone edge, with the masons'

  abandoned half-dressed.
- **The peat moss** is on the tops, with the peat-cutters' banks and

- **The shepherds'** folds, washfolds by the becks, and bothies.  Their

- **The pass road** climbs east, over the packhorse bridge on the beck,

- **The bog**, and the bog lights over it.

### The rim

- **The grazing**: short grass, the best in the dale, and the

- **The rag-trees** by the springs at the foot of the scars.

### How Kettle lives

**Who holds what.**

- **The floor** is the Guild's.  The charter lets the drainers work the
  ground they drain, so the farms are let by the Guild to tenants, and
  the rent is paid in grain to the Guild's granary.
- **What lies beneath** is the Guild's too: the pit, and every outcrop
  where the seams come up at the floor's edge, where the drains first
  turned coal up.
- **The well** is common to all, by the charter.
- **Hagg Wood** is the town's common.  Each household's cut is drawn
  by lot.
- **The High Moss** is the hill folk's, and the town buys its peat.
- **The river** is free, for eels and trout.

**Why the spring town ails.**  Kettle is not a spring town but a pit
town that grew round a spring, and in the Brand's years the spring is
part of what ails it:

- **Water feeds nobody.**  The well keeps two thousand people alive; it
  does not keep them fed, warm or paid.  That was the coal's work, and
  the pit is stopped.
- **The stone drank the pit.**  Drinking harder under the Brand, it
  drew more water through the workings than the engine was built for,
  and the engine broke under it.
- **It drinks the dale too.**  Its draw does not stop at the dale's
  edge.  The yard wells fall, the becks run low and the harvest is
  thin, while the well-head runs as it always has.
- **The well is common; its worth is not.**  With the country thirsty,
  the water is an asset, and an asset is taken.  The Guild feeds the
  town on credit borrowed against selling the town's water east, and
  its bailiffs and distrainers are in the dale because of what the
  dale holds.

Everyone else is thirsty, so Kettle's spring is worth taking.

**Who feeds the town.**  A pit town of two thousand grew past what the
dale's fields feed.  The difference was meal, salt and dried fish
bought over the Nick with coal money, and the coal money has stopped.
Now:

- **The Guild's shop**, on credit, feeds most of the town.  Its stock
  is the granary's rent grain and goods brought over the Nick on the
  League's credit, which the League extends because the holdwater will
  pay.  So the Guild feeds the town on money borrowed against selling
  the town's water.
- **The farms** feed themselves first and sell the rest for coin or
  barter.  This year's harvest is thin with the drought.
- **The river** gives eels in their autumn run downstream, and trout.
- **The hill folk** sell mutton and peat, fewer since the Guild's men
  drove them off the rim.
- **Pedlars and carriers** sell for coin until the Nick closes.
- **The chapel** keeps a soup pot from what the farms give.

**Where the coal is.**  Kettle sits on coal, and none of it is the
town's to burn:

- **The face** is under water in the drowned lower galleries.
- **The upper galleries** are worked out.  What is left is the pillars
  that hold the roof, and robbing them brings the roof down.
- **The coal yard** went east for the debt.
- **The outcrops** are the Guild's by the charter.  A man with a spade
  could dig them; a man who does is a thief, and the distrainers see
  to it.  Some dig by night.
- **The Tip** has small coal through its shale, and the poor pick it
  out by hand, a sack a day.

**The clock.**  The first snow closes the Nick, some ten weeks after
the game begins.  After it no carrier comes, and the Guild's shop has
only what it holds.  Without salt the autumn killing cannot be cured,
and there is no meat after midwinter.  Without coal a frost hollow is
killing cold, and the moss's peat was cut for the hill folk, not for
two thousand people.  The town gets through the autumn as it is; it
does not get through the winter.  The Guild's dig on the rim has the
same clock.

**After the ledger.**  Laid on the Well Stone, the ledger ends the
Guild's charter, and what it held passes to the town: the floor and
its rents, the granary, what lies beneath, and the outcrops, which any
household may then dig.  The Guild's shop shuts, and the League's
credit with it.  The town loses its supplier and gains its larder.

**After the stone.**  With the holdwater laid in the Well Stone, the
pit is lost under the water, and Kettle shrinks back to what the dale
can feed: the farms, wool, stone, lime, leather, charcoal, and coal dug
at the outcrops for its own hearths, not for the League.  Some families
go over the Nick and do not come back.  The dale keeps its water.

### What the town needs now

Three weeks without the pit, the town is short of:

- **Fuel.**  No coal for the town.  Peat from the moss, charcoal from
  the wood and what the Tip gives, carried in by hand and horse, are all
  the town burns: enough to cook, not enough to work the forge full,
  and nothing like the furnace or the engine.
- **Water.**  The yard wells are falling.  The well-head runs through
  the Guild's pump.
- **Money.**  No wages, and the Guild's shop on credit.
- **Hands.**  Families are going over the pass before the snow.

To send anyone down the pit now:

- **A way down**: the winding shaft, and the gin horses fed.
- **Light**: the chandler's tallow, and the lamp room's count kept.
- **Air**: the furnace shaft's fire lit again with charcoal, or the
  upper workings go bad.
- **Props** from the sawyers, **rope** from the ropemaker, and **food**
  from the baker and the farms.

To start the pumps again:

- **Fuel** for the engine, more than the dale has without the pit.
- **The broken part.**  Hollis knew the engine, and he is below.  The
  smith can forge and the furnace can cast, but nobody above knows
  what broke.
- **Leathers** for the pump buckets, from the tanner.
- **Or the old way**: the drainers' horse pumps, which need only horses
  and fodder.  They cannot reach the pit's depth, but they can hold the
  top of the water.

### Design notes for the town

What a believable Kettle needs on the map, roughly in order of what it
does for the town.

- **The work and the water lay it out.**  The main street runs from
  the square to the pithead, the farm road the other way.  Roads cross
  the screens' edges where the next screen agrees.  Water is the story:
  the well-head in the square, troughs, the race and the river at the
  low end.
- **Who lives where.**  Miners' terraces near the pithead; the Guild's
  larger houses and its hall near the square; the poor at the edges and
  down by the water; gardens and sheds where the town thins into the
  farms.
- **Landmarks to go into.**  The pump house, the Guild hall, the council
  house, the inn, the smithy, the engine house, a chapel, and the
  graveyard with the firedamp shift in it.  Most doors stay shut; the
  few that matter open.
- **Materials, then adornment.**  Gritstone for the Guild and the public
  buildings, brick and stone-slate for the rows; then what people add:
  painted doors, the inn's sign, window boxes, a notice board.
- **Its state tells the story.**  Shuttered doors, a spoil heap, the
  engine house with a cold chimney, no smoke over a cold town, and the
  Candle tall.

- **The Candle is a place.**  It has a screen of its own: the clough,
  the fall, rags on the thorns, coin in the pool, and the flame, which
  shows how the water stands below.

What the engine needs for these, most effect first: roads that meet at
the screens' edges; features, single things such as the well-head, a
trough, a notice board or the Candle, each a glyph with a look; named
interiors on chosen town doors; colour by building or door, not only
by band; and errand givers placed where their trade is, not by their
place in the chain.
